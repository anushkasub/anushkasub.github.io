As one walks into the Arm office in Bangalore, it is hard to overlook the photograph of a barn captioned “This is where it all started”, that pays homage to Arm’s humble beginnings in the English countryside. To me, this was reminiscent of the familiar account of startups emerging from Silicon Valley garages to disrupt entire industries. Yet beneath this familiar surface, there is a distinctiveness that is unique to Arm's story. This became evident to me through hallway conversations and Google searches (in a pre-GPT world!) that gradually revealed the strategic bets, intentional execution, and commitment to partnership that have made Arm a giant in the modern computing industry.

This creditable journey finds a storyteller in James Ashton, a financial journalist whose book ‘The Everything Blueprint’ is a mainstream telling of Arm’s journey through the decades. The book is one of broad appeal – there is something in it for almost everyone. In fact, a seasoned architect who recommended this book to me, mentioned that he discovered new insights in its pages despite having been at the company for many years!

The book, published in 2023, offers a comprehensive view of Arm’s history, from its founding and early growth in the face of stiff competition, to recent events up till the attempted acquisition by NVIDIA. It provides a “behind-the-scenes” perspective into pivotal moments, backed with geopolitical and industrial context that make it an engaging read. I was pleasantly surprised to find that the book touched upon how Arm develops and deploys the architecture today, mentioning the products we build at a high level.

As Arm undergoes its modern “re-founding”, the book offered insight into historical moments when clarity of purpose and a collaborative spirit turned ambition into lasting market leadership. One notable example was the chapter detailing how Arm successfully found its way into Nokia phones in the ‘90s, highlighting the evolution of the Thumb extension in Armv7 and a key manufacturing license with Texas Instruments. The principle of “selling a roadmap, not a product”, to quote Richard G, was particularly evident in this context. Arm-based designs transitioned seamlessly from feature phones to smartphones, even as many of the original feature phone manufacturers struggled to survive the smartphone revolution.

The content of the book is well-researched and I believe is largely aligned with Arm’s own narrative. I particularly enjoyed the author’s writing style, which included several intelligent analogies, such as one that described his process – “I have felt like a chipmaker – poring over intricate plans and etching ever more information into a relatively minute space”.

‘The Everything Blueprint’ was a read that was as informative as it was compelling. A study of corporate Darwinism in the computing industry, there were recurring threads about countries striving for silicon self-sufficiency and companies tending towards vertical integration – an especially relevant set of observations today.

To end where I began – the book also provided details about the barn “where it all started”. As it turns out, this was called Harvey’s Barn, and was once part of a farm that raised 25,000 turkeys every year. Due to subsequent regulatory changes, the barn transitioned from nurturing poultry to nurturing innovation – becoming an office for 13 pioneering engineers who were building a company called Advanced RISC Machines. Talk about an upgrade!
